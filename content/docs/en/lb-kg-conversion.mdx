One pound is **exactly 0.45359237 kg** — that's a definition (the 1959
international agreement), not a measurement. So `kg = lb × 0.45359237` and
`lb = kg ÷ 0.45359237` are always right; the only error comes from rounding
the answer.

For the gym floor you rarely need all eight decimals. Divide pounds by
**2.2** to get kilos, multiply kilos by **2.2** to get pounds — under 1% off,
which is well inside the noise of a warm-up set.

<Callout type="tip">
  Even quicker for lb → kg: halve the number, then take off 10%. 200 lb → 100
  → 90 kg (true answer 90.7).
</Callout>

## Why plates never match

Plates aren't converted, they're manufactured in whole units of each system —
so a "same" bar weighs slightly different amounts in a pound gym and a kilo gym.

- A **45 lb** plate is **20.41 kg**; a **20 kg** plate is **44.09 lb**.
- A competition **Olympic bar is 20 kg = 44.09 lb**, not 45 — many US gyms use
  a 45 lb (20.41 kg) bar instead.
- **225 lb is 102.06 kg**, and **100 kg is 220.46 lb**. The "two plates"
  milestone is not the same lift on both sides of the Atlantic.

If you're logging a lift or reporting bodyweight, use the exact number. If
you're picking a warm-up, round to whatever your rack actually has.
