**Insulin** is the hormone your pancreas releases when you eat, mostly in
response to carbohydrate and, to a lesser degree, protein. Its job is traffic
control: it tells muscle, liver and fat cells to take glucose and other
nutrients out of the blood and use or store them, and it briefly pauses the
release of stored fat while a meal is being absorbed.

**Insulin sensitivity** is how strongly your cells answer that signal. Sensitive
tissue clears a meal with a little insulin; resistant tissue needs more, so the
pancreas works harder and blood glucose stays up for longer. It isn't a fixed
trait you either have or don't: it moves with how you trained, slept and ate
this week, and muscle is the main place it plays out, because muscle takes up
most of the glucose you clear after a meal.

<Callout type="info">
  The plain version: good sensitivity means your muscles soak up the carbs you
  eat readily; poor sensitivity means the body needs more insulin to do the
  same job.
</Callout>

## What moves it

| Improves it                                | Worsens it                                   |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| Training: lifting and cardio both work     | Sitting most of the day                      |
| More muscle: a bigger place to put glucose | Gaining fat, especially around the waist     |
| Losing excess fat                          | A long, oversized surplus (a dirty bulk)     |
| 7–9 h of continuous sleep                  | Short or broken sleep, even for one night    |
| Daily steps and breaks from long sitting   | Chronic stress                               |
| A fiber-rich, mostly whole-food diet       | A diet built on ultra-processed food         |

Some of it is outside your control: genetics, age, some medications (steroids,
for example) and conditions such as PCOS.

**Training is the strongest lever you control.** A working muscle pulls glucose
in through a route that doesn't even need insulin, and that effect peaks in the
hours after a session. The muscle then stays more insulin-sensitive for up to a
day or two, which is why training regularly matters more than any single meal:
three or four sessions a week beat one brutal one.

**Daily movement fills the gaps.** When healthy young men cut their steps to
about 1,500 a day for two weeks, their insulin sensitivity fell and fat around
their organs rose. Steps, breaks from long sitting and the 150–300 minutes of
moderate activity a week that public-health guidelines advise keep glucose
uptake going between sessions.

**Body fat sets the baseline.** As fat mass grows, and the waist in particular,
sensitivity tends to fall; losing excess fat is the most reliable way to raise
it again. In people at high risk of type 2 diabetes, losing about 7% of
bodyweight and walking around 150 minutes a week cut new cases by more than
half over roughly three years.

**Sleep shows up fast.** A single night of about four hours makes healthy
adults measurably more insulin resistant the next morning, and a few short
nights in a row make glucose handling clearly worse. It recovers when sleep
does, which is one more reason to protect it
([Improve your sleep quality](/docs/sleep-quality)).

## Why it matters for lifters

**In a bulk.** How a surplus splits between muscle and fat (partitioning)
depends mostly on the training stimulus, your protein, the size of the surplus
and how lean you start. Insulin sensitivity is part of why lean, trained,
active people tend to split a surplus better. A dirty bulk works against you
twice: more fat directly, and falling sensitivity as fat builds up. It's one
reason to keep the surplus small, cap the bulk before body fat climbs too high
(see [Cut, bulk or maintain?](/docs/choosing-your-phase)), and keep around
7,000–8,000 steps a day and a little cardio while you gain.

**In a cut.** The deficit, the fat you lose and the training you keep all
improve sensitivity, one of the health wins of getting leaner whatever the
scale says. Keep carbs as high as your calories allow and put them where they
help most: around training, where they fuel the session and refill glycogen.

## Carbs around training

- Put a good share of the day's carbs in the meal one to three hours before
  training and in the one after it.
- You don't need to sprint for a shake. Uptake is fastest in the first hours
  after a hard session and stays raised into the next day, so total daily carbs
  and protein matter far more than the exact minute.
- Training in the evening? Then dinner is your post-workout meal, and carbs
  there are a good idea, not a problem.
- Timing matters more when you train twice a day or do long endurance work,
  because glycogen has less time to refill.

## Myths worth dropping

**"Insulin is the fat hormone, so carbs make you fat."** Insulin does help store
energy after a meal, but fat stored after meals is released again between them.
Over days, fat balance follows energy balance. In controlled feeding studies,
cutting carbs didn't produce more fat loss than cutting fat at equal calories,
and in a year-long trial of over 600 adults, low-fat and low-carb diets lost
similar weight, with insulin response failing to predict who did better on
which.

**"Carbs at night turn into fat."** The clock doesn't change the 24-hour
balance. Glucose tolerance is somewhat lower late in the evening, which is a
good reason to keep the last meal moderate and a couple of hours before bed,
not a reason to fear carbs at dinner.

**"Any insulin spike stops fat loss."** Spikes are normal meal physiology.
Protein raises insulin too (whey quite a lot), and nobody blames chicken breast
for fat gain. Fat loss tracks the daily deficit.

**"You need low-carb or fasting to fix it."** Fat loss, training and sleep
improve sensitivity whatever the macro split, and fasting's benefits come
mostly from the deficit it creates. Low-carb can be a valid tool, especially for
people managing diabetes with their doctor, but it isn't required.

**"Avoid fruit, it's sugar."** Whole fruit comes with fiber and water, and
eating it is linked with better, not worse, metabolic health.

**"You can feel it."** Sleepiness after meals, cravings or a belly are not a
diagnosis. Insulin resistance is measured with blood tests and read by a
doctor (see below).

**"A supplement fixes it."** Nothing in a bottle comes close to training,
walking, sleep and losing excess fat.

## When to see a doctor

This guide is about training and eating well, not diagnosis. Insulin
resistance often has no symptoms; it's picked up with blood tests a doctor
orders (fasting glucose, HbA1c, sometimes a glucose tolerance test). Ask for a
check if:

- You have risk factors: a waist more than half your height (try the
  [waist-to-height calculator](/tools/waist-to-height-ratio-calculator)), a
  parent or sibling with type 2 diabetes, past gestational diabetes, PCOS, high
  blood pressure or high triglycerides.
- You notice possible signs: unusual thirst, peeing a lot, unexplained weight
  loss, constant tiredness, blurred vision, cuts that heal slowly, or dark,
  velvety patches of skin on the neck or armpits.

<Callout type="warning">
  If you already have diabetes or take glucose-lowering medication, don't
  change your carbs, fasting or training load without your care team: low blood
  sugar is a real risk.
</Callout>
