Hours in bed are only half the story. Seven to nine hours broken by three or
four wake-ups don't recover you the way the clock suggests. Good nights start
during the day: eating well and actually moving are the base, and the habits
below build on it.

It matters for more than mood. In one well-known trial, dieters sleeping about
5.5 hours lost the same weight as those sleeping 8.5, but much less of it was
fat and more of it was lean mass. Short nights also raise next-day hunger (people
eat a few hundred kcal more on average), lower
[insulin sensitivity](/docs/insulin-sensitivity) and make every set feel
heavier.

## Are you getting enough?

You probably are if you:

- Fall asleep within 10–20 minutes, without collapsing the moment you lie down.
- Wake near your alarm, or without one, and feel rested within half an hour.
- Get through the afternoon without fighting sleep or leaning on caffeine.
- Sleep about the same on free days. Two extra hours at the weekend means a
  weekday debt.

## The protocol at a glance

| Habit         | Target                                    |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Wake time     | The same every day, weekends within ~1 h  |
| Morning light | 10–30 min outdoors, early                 |
| Caffeine      | Last dose ~8 h before bed                 |
| Alcohol       | None in the last 3–4 h, and less overall  |
| Last meal     | Finished 2–3 h before bed                 |
| Screens       | Last hour (two if you can) as a wind-down |
| Room          | Dark, quiet and cool, ~16–19 °C           |
| Naps          | 10–20 min, before mid-afternoon           |

## 1. Anchor your wake time

Regularity beats extra hours. Pick a wake time you can keep seven days a week
and let bedtime follow: go to bed when sleepy, roughly 7.5–9 hours before the
alarm. For most work schedules that lands bedtime between about 22:00 and
midnight, but a consistent window matters more than a specific clock. Sleeping
in three hours every weekend is like flying across time zones twice a week.

## 2. Get daylight early

Outdoor light soon after waking is the strongest signal for your body clock: it
makes you alert earlier in the day and sleepy earlier at night. Even a cloudy
morning outside is many times brighter than indoor lighting. Ten to thirty
minutes is enough: a walk, coffee on the balcony, part of the commute on foot
(which also counts as steps). In the evening do the opposite: dimmer, warmer
lights.

## 3. Set a caffeine cut-off

Caffeine's half-life is around five hours and varies a lot between people, so
half of a 16:00 coffee is still around at 21:00. In a lab study, a large dose
taken six hours before bed still cut measured sleep by more than an hour, and
people didn't notice. Make your last caffeine about eight hours before bed,
around 15:00 for a 23:00 bedtime, and count everything: pre-workout, energy
drinks, cola, strong tea and "fat burners". Lifting late? Skip the stimulant or
use a caffeine-free pre-workout.

## 4. Keep alcohol away from bedtime

Alcohol helps you fall asleep, then fragments the second half of the night and
cuts REM sleep, so you wake more and recover less. Leave at least three to four
hours between the last drink and bed, and keep it small. If you drink, drink
earlier, with food and water.

## 5. Make the room dark, quiet and cool

- **Dark:** blackout curtains or an eye mask; cover standby lights.
- **Quiet:** earplugs or a steady background noise.
- **Cool:** around 16–19 °C suits most people; being too hot is one of the most
  common reasons for broken sleep.

A warm shower or bath one to two hours before bed helps you fall asleep a little
faster: afterwards the skin sheds heat and your core temperature drops, which is
one of the body's own signals for sleep.

## 6. Finish eating two to three hours before bed

A large, late or very fatty meal can mean reflux and restless sleep, and going
to bed starving doesn't help either. Make dinner the lightest main meal and
finish it two to three hours before bed. Hungry later? A small protein snack
(yogurt, cottage cheese, a glass of milk) is fine. Drink well through the day
and taper in the last hour or two, so you don't wake up to pee.

## 7. Give the last hour to winding down

Screens hit twice: the light nudges your body clock later, and the content keeps
your brain engaged and pushes bedtime back ("one more episode"). Night mode and
blue-light glasses are cheap and harmless, but the evidence that they fix sleep
is weak; putting the phone away is the bigger win. Make the last hour (two if
you can manage it) screen-free: read on paper, stretch or do some mobility,
shower, and write tomorrow's to-do list so it stops circling in your head.
Charge the phone out of reach.

## 8. Train, but not right before bed

Regular training and an active day improve sleep. Evening sessions are fine for
most people; only hard sessions ending within about an hour of bed tend to
delay sleep. If you train late, finish one to two hours before bed, skip the
stimulant and eat your post-workout meal soon after.

## 9. Nap smart

A 10–20 minute nap before about 15:00 restores alertness without stealing night
sleep. Longer or later naps can make it harder to fall asleep at night; if you
struggle at night, skip them for a while.

## If you can't fall asleep

Don't fight it in bed. If you've been awake for what feels like twenty minutes,
get up, go somewhere dim, do something calm and come back when you feel sleepy.
Don't check the clock. This is part of the standard behavioural treatment for
insomnia, and it teaches your brain that bed means sleep.

## After a bad night

One bad night costs less than you fear; worrying about it costs more.

- **Get up at your usual time,** within about an hour. Sleeping until noon shifts
  tonight too.
- **Get outside light early,** and use caffeine in the morning only.
- **Nap if you need to,** 20 minutes before mid-afternoon.
- **Train as planned.** One short night barely moves strength. If the warm-ups
  feel bad, drop a set or stay a rep or two further from failure, and don't test
  a max that day.
- **Expect more hunger.** Plan protein and vegetables, and don't judge your diet
  on one hungry day.
- **Go to bed at your normal time,** or a little earlier if you're sleepy, not
  three hours early.

Several bad nights in a row? Go back through the protocol before reaching for
anything else.

## Sleep aids and trackers

**Melatonin** is a timing signal, not a sleeping pill. It helps most with jet lag
and shifted schedules; for everyday trouble falling asleep it shortens the wait
by minutes, not hours. If you use it, take it shortly before bed, and ask a
doctor or pharmacist first if you take other medication, are pregnant or need it
for weeks (in some countries it's prescription-only). See
[supplements](/docs/supplements) for the rest of the shelf.

**Trackers** are useful for checking bed and wake times. Their sleep-stage
numbers are estimates, so don't chase a score; how you feel and perform is the
better read.

## When to see a doctor

- Loud snoring with gasping, choking or pauses in breathing (possible sleep
  apnea, more common with higher bodyweight and a thicker neck).
- Sleepiness during the day despite enough hours, or dozing off while driving.
- Trouble sleeping at least three nights a week for three months or more.
  Chronic insomnia has an effective first-line treatment (cognitive behavioural
  therapy for insomnia), and it isn't a sleeping pill.
- Restless, crawling legs at night, or low mood and anxiety that keep you up.

<Callout type="tip">
  Fix the wake time first. Most of the other habits get easier once your body
  knows when the day starts.
</Callout>
