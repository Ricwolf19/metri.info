Ectomorph, mesomorph, endomorph: you've probably been told which one you are,
and that it decides how you should eat and train. It doesn't. A body-type label
describes what your body looks like today. It doesn't explain why, and it
changes as you train and eat.

## What the labels claim

| Type      | The classic description                     | What "type" plans prescribe  |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Ectomorph | Long, narrow, lean; "can't gain weight"     | Lots of carbs, little cardio |
| Mesomorph | Broad, muscular; "gains muscle, stays lean" | Almost anything works        |
| Endomorph | Rounder, wider; "gains fat easily"          | Low-carb, lots of cardio     |

## Where they came from

The types were coined in the 1940s by the American psychologist William
Sheldon, who rated standardized photographs of thousands of college students.
He named his three components after the embryo's tissue layers (endoderm for
the gut, mesoderm for muscle and bone, ectoderm for skin and nerves), assuming
each physique showed which layer "dominated". His real interest was
temperament: he claimed each body came with a personality, and later linked
physique to delinquency. Those claims didn't hold up, and psychology dropped
the theory.

What survived is a descriptive version. The Heath–Carter method rates a body on
three scales from measurements (skinfolds, bone widths, girths, height and
weight), producing a score such as 3–5–2:

| Component  | What it rates                          | Changes with          |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Endomorphy | Relative fatness                       | Diet and activity     |
| Mesomorphy | Muscle and bone relative to height     | Training and protein  |
| Ectomorphy | Linearity: height relative to mass     | Weight gained or lost |

Fat and muscle drive two of the three ratings, so the score moves when they do.
Sports scientists use it to describe athletes at one moment, as a description
of the current body that changes with training, diet and age. Nobody in that
field uses it to write a diet.

## Why it's not a plan

1. **It's a snapshot, not a cause.** The type is read off your current fat,
   muscle and frame, so "endomorph" mostly means "carrying more fat right now".
   Saying you gain fat because you're an endomorph is circular.
2. **It changes.** Bring body fat down and muscle up and the same person reads
   as a different type. A heavy beginner gets called an endomorph; the same
   person, a few years of lifting later, gets called a mesomorph.
3. **Most people are mixtures.** Real ratings sit on a continuum; few bodies fit
   one box cleanly.
4. **The type diets are untested.** There is no controlled evidence that eating
   "for your type" beats getting calories and protein right. The closest real
   test, a year-long trial that matched people to low-fat or low-carb by their
   genes or insulin response, found the match made no difference, and the
   spread of results inside each diet was far larger than the gap between them.
5. **It talks people out of progress.** "I'm an ectomorph, I can't gain" and
   "I'm an endomorph, I'll always be fat" both stop people doing the things that
   work.

## What actually differs between people

Genetics is real. You can't change it, but you can work with it, and most of
what people blame on their "type" is something you can measure and adjust.

| What varies                 | What it means for you                                                                                |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Response to training        | Some gain faster than others; slow responders usually respond to more volume or a different program |
| Height, limbs and frame     | Leverages in the squat, bench and deadlift, and how much muscle it takes to look big                |
| Muscle shape and insertions | The shape of a biceps or calf; size can change, shape mostly can't                                   |
| Fat distribution            | Where fat goes first and leaves last; it still comes off in a deficit                                |
| Starting point              | Body fat and training age decide your first phase                                                    |
| Appetite and NEAT           | How easy a surplus or deficit feels; some people move a lot more when they eat more                  |
| Health and hormones         | Thyroid problems, PCOS or medications can matter; that's a doctor's question                         |

How much response varies is striking: in one study of almost 600 people doing
the same 12-week arm program, muscle size changed anywhere from nothing to more
than 50%. That spread is about speed, not about whether you can build muscle.

## What to do instead

Read your own data, not a label.

**Told you're an ectomorph or a "hardgainer"?** Usually that's a small appetite,
a lot of daily movement and an intake lower than it feels. Log food for two
weeks; if the weekly average isn't climbing, add about 10%. Lean on
energy-dense foods and liquid calories, and let fat sit at the top of its range.

**Told you're an endomorph?** Usually that's more body fat right now, often with
little training behind it and a low step count. Cut first if body fat is high,
walk 8,000–10,000 steps a day, build meals around protein and fiber for
fullness, and lift to keep and build muscle.

**Told you're a mesomorph?** Often that's someone with years of training
already, or a fast responder. The same rules apply, and progress slows with
experience for them too.

The method is the same for every body: estimate maintenance, pick the phase
your body fat points to, train with progressive overload, and steer by the
weekly average, the tape and photos, adjusting every two weeks.

<Callout type="tip">
  If a label makes you train less or give up on a phase, drop the label and
  keep the data.
</Callout>
