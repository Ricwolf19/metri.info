Your maintenance calories are not a fixed number. Lose weight and the body you
are feeding costs less to run; gain weight and it costs more. On top of that
size effect, the body adds a response of its own: it trims spending when food
is scarce and raises it when food is plentiful. That response is **adaptive
thermogenesis**, and it is why the deficit that worked in week one slowly
closes, and why the surplus that moved the scale in month one eventually stops.

## Where your daily burn goes

Your total daily expenditure (TDEE) has four parts, and they don't all react
the same way to a diet.

| Part                                          | Typical share | In a deficit                                       |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **BMR** (keeping you alive at rest)           | ~60–70%       | Falls a little: a smaller body, a modest slowdown  |
| **TEF** (digesting food)                      | ~10%          | Falls with the food you remove; protein costs most |
| **Exercise**                                  | Varies        | The same session costs less at a lower weight      |
| **NEAT** (steps, fidgeting, chores, standing) | Most variable | Usually falls the most, often without you noticing |

NEAT is the line to watch. In a deficit you tend to move less without deciding
to: fewer steps, more sitting, slower walking, the lift instead of the stairs.
That drop is real energy, and it is the part you can measure and take back.

Gaining works in reverse. In a classic overfeeding study, people eating an extra
1,000 kcal a day for eight weeks gained very different amounts of fat, roughly
tenfold apart, and the best predictor was how much their everyday movement went
up on its own.

## How big it really is

Two effects get lumped together under "my metabolism slowed down":

1. **The smaller-body effect.** Every kilo you lose takes some resting burn and
   some movement cost with it. That isn't adaptation, just arithmetic, and it's
   why every formula gives a lower number at a lower weight.
2. **The adaptive part.** The extra drop beyond what the lost tissue explains.
   As fat falls, so do the hormones that report it (leptin among them), thyroid
   output dips, muscle does the same work for slightly less energy, and hunger
   rises. In controlled studies the resting share of it is usually on the order
   of 100 kcal a day. Add the quiet fall in everyday movement and total
   expenditure can sit a few hundred kcal below what your new weight predicts,
   more after large, fast or very long diets.

An illustration with round numbers:

|                      | Start          | 8 kg later        |
| -------------------- | -------------- | ----------------- |
| Bodyweight           | 85 kg          | 77 kg             |
| Maintenance          | ~2,800 kcal    | ~2,500 kcal       |
| Eating 2,500 kcal is | a ~10% deficit | about maintenance |

Nothing broke. The diet worked, the body got smaller and a little more
economical, and the old deficit became the new maintenance.

The same happens when you bulk: as weight and muscle climb, the surplus that
worked becomes maintenance. If the weekly average and your lifts stall and
your waist isn't growing, it's time to add food again.

## How to respond

1. **Let the scale re-estimate for you.** Recalculate your TDEE every 4–5 kg of
   change, but trust the weekly fasted average over any formula: it already
   includes every adaptation.
2. **Adjust in small steps, every two weeks.** Cutting and the average has held
   for two weeks: take off 5–10%. Bulking and nothing moved in two weeks: add
   about 10%. Moving as planned: change nothing. Take cuts from carbs or fat,
   never from protein.
3. **Defend NEAT before you cut food.** Count your steps, hold them steady, then
   raise them gradually. Since this is where much of the drop hides, a step
   target is the most direct counter.
4. **Keep protein high and keep lifting.** Muscle is the tissue you want to keep,
   and keeping it keeps more of your expenditure. Protein also costs the most to
   digest and fills you up best.
5. **Lose at a moderate pace.** Around 0.5–1% of bodyweight a week (about 400 g
   for most people). Faster loss brings more adaptation, more hunger, worse
   sessions and more muscle lost.
6. **Plan maintenance phases.** After 8–12 weeks of dieting, one or two weeks at
   your new maintenance (a diet break), or a longer maintenance phase, gives
   training, hunger and motivation a rest. The evidence is mixed: one trial in
   men with obesity lost more fat per week of dieting with two-weeks-on,
   two-weeks-off; trials in trained lifters found similar fat loss either way.
   Use breaks for adherence, not as a metabolic reset, and expect 0.5–1 kg of
   water and glycogen back in the first days.
7. **Sleep and manage stress.** Short sleep makes a deficit cost more muscle and
   more hunger. See [Improve your sleep quality](/docs/sleep-quality).
8. **End the cut at the new maintenance.** When the diet ends, step up to where
   your weekly average holds, not back to the number you ate before the diet.
   Slow "reverse diets" have little evidence of protecting metabolism; their
   real use is finding the new maintenance without overshooting.

<Callout type="warning">
  A lost or irregular period, dizziness, feeling cold all the time or
  exhaustion during a diet are not a plateau to push through. Stop the cut, eat
  at maintenance and see a doctor.
</Callout>

## What it is not

**Not "metabolic damage".** Metabolism adapts; it doesn't break. The adaptive
part scales with how much, how fast and how long you dieted, and most of it
eases once you stop restricting. What remains while you hold a much lower
weight is mostly a lighter body needing less, plus a modest tendency to defend
lost fat. That's why keeping weight off takes active habits (steps, training,
protein), not a repair. The dramatic figures you see online, metabolisms
running ~500 kcal a day "slow" years later, come from extreme cases such as
televised weight-loss contests with huge deficits and hours of daily exercise.

**Not "starvation mode".** Eating too little does not make you stop losing fat,
let alone gain it. In the Minnesota starvation experiment of the 1940s, men on
roughly half their usual intake for six months slowed down a lot and still lost
about a quarter of their bodyweight. Adaptation narrows a deficit; it does not
reverse it. When the scale stalls in a real deficit, it is usually one of three
things: the deficit has closed (smaller body, less NEAT), intake has crept up
(untracked bites, weekends, cooking oil), or water is hiding fat loss for a week
or two (hard training blocks, stress, salt, the menstrual cycle). The second is
more common than it feels: in a classic study of people who said they couldn't
lose weight on very little food, measured intake was close to double what they
had logged.

**Not something a supplement fixes.** "Thermogenic" fat burners add little
beyond their caffeine. Nothing in a capsule undoes adaptation; steps, training,
protein and patience do the work.

<Callout type="tip">
  The scale already knows. Your weekly average includes every adaptation, so two
  clean weeks of data beat any recalculation.
</Callout>
